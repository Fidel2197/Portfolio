Next.js + AI · Food assistant

SnapChef

Turn a food photo into recipe ideas, ingredient suggestions, and nutrition estimates, with saved scans and account history.

Next.js · React · TypeScript · Supabase · AI APIs

Open app ↗View repository ↗

From a photo to something cookable

Recognizing a dish is only part of the task. Someone also needs ingredients, steps, substitutions, and a plan that fits their budget or equipment. SnapChef carries a food photo through that longer workflow, with preferences for things such as a dorm kitchen, a quick meal, or higher protein.

Where the photo goes

The browser sends the image and preferences to a Next.js server route. That route calls the configured AI provider; its key stays on the server. Supabase handles sign-in and saved recipe records, so a useful result can be reopened from My Scans instead of being lost when the page closes.

Giving failures a useful response

The client and API share a 3 MB upload limit and accept JPEG, PNG, and WebP. Empty or unsupported uploads get an explanation before analysis. Provider failures and timeouts are shown as errors, so a failed request does not quietly become a successful-looking recipe.

Making a large component easier to change

Upload controls, accounts, history, and result views now have separate components and supporting services. The 33 automated tests cover invalid uploads, request failures, saving and deleting history, cleanup after a failed save, and stale history arriving after sign-out. External AI and Supabase calls are mocked in those tests.

Being clear about estimates

A photo cannot reveal every ingredient, allergen, or portion size. Dish identification, nutrition, and prices are estimates; video links are search results. The built-in example is explicitly labeled and cannot be saved as an analyzed scan. Its purpose is to let someone understand the output before uploading their own photo.

A storage limitation to know about

Recipe rows are scoped to their owner, but the current image bucket is public: a person with a saved photo’s URL can view it. That distinction matters, so it is documented in the repository. Private image storage would require coordinated bucket-policy and signed-URL changes, not just a different label in the interface.
